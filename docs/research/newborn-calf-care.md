# What a newborn calf needs, and what the farm loses in calves

**Question:** OpenFarm records a Calving and creates each live calf as an Animal, but the Standard Playbook has no calf care. It will raise newborn work per calf (colostrum, navel, weigh-in), a first deworming and the calf vaccines, and Weaning at 90 days, and it will report calf losses. What should each Step say, and on what source? How soon, and how much, colostrum? How is the navel treated? What do Bangladeshi calves weigh at birth, to set the weigh-in's warn range? When should a calf be wormed against _Toxocara vitulorum_, and with what? At what ages does DLS vaccinate calves? When do Bangladeshi farms wean, and on how much milk? How many calves do Bangladeshi dairy farms lose, to what, and at what age? (Plan: `.scratch/openfarm-calf-care/README.md`.)

## Answer

1. **Colostrum: aim for the first feed within 2 hours; 6 hours is the outer limit.**
   - Godden (2019): "Producers should aim to feed all calves within 1 to 2 hours after birth." Teagasc and Wisconsin say the same.
   - Merck: at least 3 L of good colostrum "within 6 hours after birth". NG-GLPP: "within 4-6 hours of birth", and "ideally … within 30 minutes".
   - The gut closes at about 24 hours (Godden). Absorption fell from 51.8% at 45 minutes to 35.6% at 6 hours (Fischer 2018, abstract only).
2. **How much: about 10% of birth weight at the first feed.** NG-GLPP says "approximately 2 liters or the amount on its 10% body weight". Godden says 10–12%; a 2025 trial found 8–10% the best compromise (Frederick, abstract only). That is **3.0 L for a 30 kg crossbred calf, 2.0 L for a 20 kg calf and 1.5 L for a 15 kg deshi calf** (my arithmetic).
3. **Second feed within 12 hours, then colostrum for 3 days.** NG-GLPP asks for "about 4 liters of colostrum intake within 12 hours" and colostrum "for at least 3 days (72 hours)". Merck puts the second feed "between 8 and 12 hours after birth"; Wisconsin says "an additional 2 liters within 12 hours".
4. **Quality: Brix ≥22% marks good first-feed colostrum.** Godden gives "IgG ≥50 g/L or Brix ≥22%"; Merck and Teagasc agree. NG-GLPP gives only an IgG figure (">25g/L", "acceptable level is 50g/L"). **Method:** bottle or oesophageal tube give "equal and acceptable levels of passive transfer", provided the calf gets the volume (Godden). Tube any calf that will not drink it (Penn State, Teagasc).
5. **Delay costs health, but the "10% per hour" figure was not found in any primary source.**
   - **What was found:** failed passive transfer doubles the odds of death: adjusted risk 2.12 (1.43–3.13) in the Raboisson 2016 meta-analysis.
   - **Per hour of delay:** each hour's delay to colostrum raised the odds of navel infection by 15% (OR 1.15, Van Camp 2022, abstract only).
   - **In Bangladesh,** 55% of 210 Chattogram farms let the calf drink within 30 minutes. Only 2 farms fed it after 2 hours (Chowdhury 2017).
6. **Navel: dip, don't spray, in 7% tincture of iodine at birth.** NG-GLPP: "The navel cord should be disinfected with 7% iodine solution immediately after birth." Dip, not spray (CalfCare.ca). Re-check at 24 hours and re-dip if needed (CalfCare.ca); "repeated after 12 hours" in an extension summary **[SNIPPET]**.
   - **The trials are weak.** One dip of 7% iodine did not reduce navel infection (20% vs 22%, Van Camp 2022, abstract only). 2% chlorhexidine did as well as 7% iodine (Wieland 2017, abstract only).
   - **The dip is cheap; hygiene and colostrum matter more.** In a Bangladeshi study navel ill hit 7.22% of calves, on farms that "did not use antiseptic" (Islam 2015).
7. **Birth weight in Bangladesh.**
   - **HF × Local crossbreds:** means 24.6–27.2 kg, observed range **15–40 kg** (Hridoy 2025, 4,370 calves). Registered 75% Friesian calves averaged 29.3 kg (Rahman 2015).
   - **Red Chittagong:** 14.7–15.4 kg; males 16.2, females 13.9 (Nahar 2016). **Local:** 16.7 kg **[SNIPPET]**.
   - **Warn range: below 12 kg or above 45 kg** (my choice, §3). **The fattening weigh-in's 20 kg minimum must not apply to calves.**
8. **First-day checks.**
   - **Teagasc's vigour times:** head up in 3 minutes, sitting up in 5, trying to stand by 20, standing by 60–90 minutes.
   - **NG-GLPP:** suckling "within 20 min of birth" once standing; check "for atresia ani and other malformations"; watch that the calf passes dung and urine; tag each newborn ("rather with an ear tag").
9. **_Toxocara vitulorum_ reaches the calf in the dam's milk. Worm at 10–16 days with a drug that kills immature worms.**
   - **Transmission and timing:** passes in milk mostly in the first ~9 days; eggs appear in the calf's dung from about 3 weeks. That is why Roberts treats at 10–16 days, in cattle calves too (Roberts 1992, abstract only).
   - **Drugs:** against immature worms pyrantel and levamisole were 97% effective; piperazine 42% (Roberts 1989, abstract only). In Bangladeshi buffalo calves ivermectin cut egg counts 96.8%, levamisole 94.2%, albendazole 85.8% (Biswas 2022).
   - **Cattle calves are at risk too:** 25% of cattle calves in Gopalganj carried _Toxocara_, against 3.75% of adults (Mustafa 2022).
   - **Conflict:** NG-GLPP Appendix 35 gives piperazine "within 5-6 days of birth", repeated every 45 days. That is earlier than the milk-borne larvae stop, and piperazine is the weakest of the drugs Roberts tested. **The Vet should choose the product.**
10. **Vaccination ages (NG-GLPP Appendix 31, confirmed from the PDF):**
    - **FMD:** from 4 months, booster 1 month later, then six-monthly.
    - **Anthrax:** from 4 months.
    - **HS and BQ:** from 6 months each. Anthrax, HS and BQ are then yearly "in endemic areas".
    - **Brucellosis:** female calves at 4–8 months, once.
    - **Theileriosis:** from 3 months, once, crossbred and exotic cattle only.
    - **IBR:** from 3 months, booster 1 month later, then six-monthly.
    - Ages in days are in the table below (my arithmetic, 30-day months). LSD is absent from the schedule, as `bangladesh-regulatory.md` §5.4 already notes.
11. **Weaning.** NG-GLPP describes two practices:
    - **Backyard farms** wean naturally at 8–10 months. **Commercial farms** wean early, "at 100-120 kg body weight".
    - **Weaning goes by feed, not age:** "Weaning of calves should be based upon dry matter intakes", with "calf starter at 1 kg/day for 3 days before weaning". The rumen takes 3–4 months to develop.
    - **Milk before weaning:** "10% of its bwt/day for first 3 weeks", then "15% of their bwt/day after 3 weeks".
    - **Solid feed:** concentrate from 3 weeks, hay from 5–8 weeks. Water free-choice from day 3.
    - **No Bangladeshi survey was found that states the weaning age farms use.** "Generally suckling and weaning is not controlled" (Kamal 2010).
    - **90 days fits DLS's early weaning.** A 90-day crossbred calf weighs about 64 kg, not 100–120 (Rahman 2015). **So weaning at 90 days must check the starter intake, not the weight.**
12. **Calf losses in Bangladesh.**
    - **How many:** calf mortality ran 6.29% over 4 months on small CIG farms (Islam 2015) and 12.28% on 73 commercial farms in 2014 (Parvez 2020). NG-GLPP says "Above 10% of calf mortality is not acceptable".
    - **Diseases:** diarrhoea is the commonest, then navel ill and pneumonia (34.8%, 7.2% and 6.3% of calves, Islam 2015).
    - **Causes of death:** pneumonia leads, with gut infections after it (Hossain 2013: respiratory 38.8% of deaths, alimentary 16.7%).
    - **Age and season:** the first month is the deadliest in every study. Winter was worst at CCBDF (38.0% of deaths); monsoon was worst at Bogra (36.4%) **[SNIPPET]**.
    - **Correction to the plan:** its "30.9% morbidity and 8.64% mortality" come from **Ethiopia** (Tora 2021, Gamo zone), not Bangladesh.

## 1. Colostrum

**When.**

- **NG-GLPP §11.2** (p. 156):
  - (b) "Colostrum should be fed to a calf approximately 2 liters or the amount on its 10% body weight within 4-6 hours of birth."
  - (c) "Ideally, the colostrum feeding should be started to a calf within 30 minutes of birth and ensured about 4 liters of colostrum intake within 12 hours."
  - (f) "It should be fed to all newborns for at least 3 days (72 hours)."
  - (h) "The newborn can be fed colostrum by bottle feeding or by suckling mother."

  §11.1(j) adds that once the calf "stands up from the ground, then the newborn should be allowed to suckle its mother within 20 min of birth". §12.1.1.1.1(b) repeats "about 4 liters colostrum within 12 hours" and adds: "Calves not received recommended dose of colostrum grow at only 2/3rd compared to that received recommended dose of colostrum on time."

- **Godden 2019:** "Producers should aim to feed all calves within 1 to 2 hours after birth." Absorption "decreases linearly with time from birth to completely close at approximately 24 hours". Delaying the first feed "can only slightly postpone gut closure (36 hours)".
- **Merck (Dec 2025):** "all calves should receive at least 3 L of high-quality colostrum (IgG concentration > 50 mg/mL) within 6 hours after birth". The second feeding comes "between 8 and 12 hours after birth"; "4 L (1 gallon) … within 6 hours" is the alternative.
- **Teagasc** calls the first hour the "Golden Hour". Its "Colostrum 1-2-3" rule (from Animal Health Ireland) is: first milking, within two hours, at least three litres.
- **Wisconsin:** "within the first 2 hours of life"; after four hours absorption declines, "ending completely by 24 hours".
- **Penn State's own barn:** "Feed one gallon of colostrum within 2 hours after birth. If the calf does not drink the colostrum it will need to be tube-fed."

**The farm's settled 2 hours is the consensus target. 6 hours is the outer limit** (Merck; NG-GLPP's "4-6 hours").

**How much.**

| Source                         | First feed                                                                                              |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| NG-GLPP §11.2(b)               | "approximately 2 liters or … 10% body weight"                                                           |
| Godden 2019                    | "10% to 12% of their body weight (BW) of colostrum at first feeding (3–4 L for a Holstein calf)"        |
| Wisconsin                      | "8%-10% of their body weight … 3-4 liters at birth, plus an additional 2 liters within 12 hours"        |
| Teagasc                        | "8.5% of their birth bodyweight for their first feed (e.g. 35 kg calf requires three litres)"           |
| CalfCare.ca                    | "about 15 per cent of its bodyweight in colostrum the first day divided into first and second feedings" |
| Frederick 2025 (abstract only) | 6/8/10/12% of birth weight: "8% or 10% … within 2 h after birth offers a compromise"                    |

In Frederick's trial, 24-hour serum IgG was 28.8, 37.4, 41.1 and 43.4 g/L for 6, 8, 10 and 12%. Absorption efficiency fell with volume (47.8 → 36.3%). "Colic-like" kicking appeared only at 10 and 12% (abstract only). Those calves were Holsteins of 40 kg on average, fed by tube.

**10% of birth weight is NG-GLPP's own rule and the middle of the evidence.**

| Birth weight | First feed at 10% (my arithmetic) | Total by 12 h for NG-GLPP's "about 4 L"                     |
| ------------ | --------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| 15 kg deshi  | 1.5 L                             | 4 L is 27% of weight: too much for this calf (my inference) |
| 20 kg        | 2.0 L                             | —                                                           |
| 25 kg        | 2.5 L                             | —                                                           |
| 30 kg cross  | 3.0 L                             | 4 L ≈ 13%, near CalfCare's 15% per day                      |

NG-GLPP's "4 liters within 12 hours" is written for a crossbred or exotic calf. **For the second feed, about 5% of birth weight** fits Godden's cited trial ("a second feeding (5% BW) … at 12 hours of age"). It also brings the first day to about 15%, CalfCare.ca's figure (my inference).

**Second feed.** Godden: "Providing a second feeding sometime after the first postnatal meal can further increase passive transfer of IgG." Robbers 2021: "an additional feeding within 12 h significantly increased the serum immunoglobulin content". In von Riedheim 2026 (abstract only), a second colostrum meal at 24 h gave lower IgG than one at 12 h (16.9 vs 23.3 mg/mL).

**Quality.**

- **Godden:** "a value between 18% and 23% Brix is an appropriate cut point for determining good-quality colostrum (IgG >50 g/L)"; "An achievable herd-level goal is to harvest high-quality colostrum (IgG ≥50 g/L or Brix ≥22%) in greater than or equal to 90% of samples tested".
- **Merck:** "Brix value > 22% is associated with colostrum IgG concentration of 50 mg/mL or higher".
- **Teagasc:** "A score of 22% is the cut off". Wisconsin's is stricter: ">25%" (75 g/L).

A Brix refractometer is a pocket instrument. Whether the farm owns one is a question for the Owner. **The Step can ask for the reading and say what to do below 22%**; NG-GLPP's advice to refrigerate spare colostrum gives the farm a store to draw on.

**Method.**

- **Godden:** "equal and acceptable levels of passive transfer are achieved when colostrum is delivered by nipple bottle or esophageal tube feeder, provided that a sufficient volume of colostrum is delivered". Producers feeding by bottle "should be prepared to deliver any remaining colostrum using a tube feeder, or provide a second bottle feeding within 6 hours".
- **Teagasc:** "Calves left to suckle the dam are less likely to receive adequate transfer of immunity."

In Chattogram 60% of farms used suckling (Chowdhury 2017). **So "fed by suckling" should be a way to record the feed, but it cannot confirm the volume.**

**Delay → illness and death.**

- **Fischer 2018** (abstract only): a 45-minute, 6-hour or 12-hour first feed gave maximum absorption efficiency of 51.8%, 35.6% and 35.1%.
- **Robbers 2021**, from its cited models: "calf serum IgG concentration decreased with 2 mg/ml with every 30 min delay", or "0.32 mg/ml with each hour".
- **Raboisson 2016:** "The adjusted risks (and 95% confidence intervals) for mortality, bovine respiratory disease, diarrhoea and overall morbidity in the case of FPT were 2.12 (1.43–3.13), 1.75 (1.50–2.03), 1.51 (1.05–2.17) and 1.91 (1.63–2.24)."
- **Van Camp 2022** (abstract only): "for every additional hour that calves received colostrum after birth, the odds of developing an external umbilical infection increased during the first month of life (odds ratio = 1.15)".
- **The "each hour of delay … 10%" figure** came up only in a search engine's summary, attributed to "Bangladesh dairy farms". No paper behind it was found. Not used.
- **Bangladesh (Chowdhury 2017, Table 3):** first colostrum "Within 30 minutes 115 (55%)", "Within 2 hours 90", "More than 2 hours 4". "Only 2 farms out of 210 sampled farms provided the calves with colostrums after 2 hours of birth." In the same survey, 66% of farms separated the calf more than 24 hours after birth.

## 2. Navel care

**DLS.** NG-GLPP §11.1 (p. 155):

- (h) "The navel cord should be disinfected with 7% iodine solution immediately after birth."
- (i) "The naval cord should be cut 1" below from the navel with a sterilized blade or scissors and be sanitized the navel and cord with Tincture iodine or with a suitable antiseptic."

**Extension.**

- **CalfCare.ca:**
  - Use "Tincture of iodine (at least seven per cent iodine)"; "Don't use a diluted iodine solution such as teat dip".
  - "Don't use a spray ‐ it won't cover the full cord area, nor travel up the openings of the blood vessels as well as a dipped product will."
  - Dip "immediately, once the calf is breathing well on its own". Re-check at 24 hours, "Re‐dip the navel if necessary", and check "at least once daily for the first week".
  - "Change the dipping solution after five calves or two days, whichever comes first."
- **Penn State's barn:** "Dip the navel generously with 7% iodine making sure to get good coverage."
- **An extension summary:** dip in "7% or higher tincture of iodine … and repeated after 12 hours" **[SNIPPET]**.

**Trials.**

- **Van Camp 2022** (abstract only; 284 Holstein calves, Ontario): a single 7% iodine dip left 20% with external umbilical infection, against 22% of undipped calves. There was "no effect of treatment", and "Farm-level management factors, including colostrum management, appear to have more influence".
- **Wieland 2017** (abstract only): Navel Guard, 7% iodine and 2% chlorhexidine showed "no difference in the effectiveness of the 3 treatments in the prevention of umbilical infection, pneumonia, diarrhea, or arthritis".
- **Teagasc** says to use antisepsis "(chlorhexidine or iodine) if navel ill is a problem on farm and hygiene is already optimal". Where navel ill persists, "stop cord dressing altogether and focus instead on optimising calf immunity and calving hygiene".

**For this farm.** Keep the dip: DLS recommends it, it is cheap, and navel ill is common in Bangladesh. Islam 2015 found 7.22% of calves with navel ill, where farms "did not cut umbilical cord just after delivery and did not use antiseptic". The Owner's "at birth and again within the day" matches CalfCare.ca's re-check and the snippet's 12 hours. **The evidence is for the dip as part of hygiene, not as a cure.**

**Navel ill signs.**

- **Teagasc:** check "for excessive bleeding, pain, abnormal swelling, odour or pus" in the first week.
- **CalfCare.ca:** "no drainage or foul smell, nor should palpation cause a painful response".
- **Van Camp's definition:** "an enlarged umbilicus with pain, heat, or a malodorous discharge".

**Joint ill is its sequel.** Teagasc: "The spread of infection from the environment into the calf via the navel cord is the cause of navel or joint ill." Islam 2015 recorded arthritis in 3.63% of calves.

**Warn range (my choice): below 12 kg or above 45 kg.**

## 4. Other first-day checks

**Breathing.** NG-GLPP §11.1:

- (d) "Newborn must respire after clearing the mucus and other filths from its nostrils."
- (e) If it does not breathe, "it should be shaken gently by holding its head down to clear airways".
- (g) Its resuscitation steps: dry, warm (especially at a rectal temperature of 97 °F), "sniffing" position, clear the mouth and nose, "insert a straw into its nose to stimulate sneezing and breathing".

Teagasc lists the signs of a calf needing resuscitation: "lack or absence of breathing, gasping or bellowing, lying flat, unable to lift the head, slow to sit up, stand and to suck". It says to suspend the calf upside down "never longer than one minute".

**Standing and suckling.** Teagasc's "Vigour Indicators", in minutes after calving:

| Sign                   | Minutes |
| ---------------------- | ------- |
| Lifts its head         | 3       |
| Sits up on its brisket | 5       |
| Attempts to stand      | 20      |
| Stands on its own      | 60–90   |

NG-GLPP §11.1(j) has the calf suckle "within 20 min of birth" once it stands. That is tighter than Teagasc's standing times; **a calf not up and sucking by 2 hours is also the calf whose colostrum is late.**

**Other checks in NG-GLPP §11.1:**

- (k) "examined after birth for atresia ani and other malformations".
- (l) "Limbs, eyes, nose, ears, teeth and gums, umbilical cord and other parts of the body should be checked for any abnormality".
- (m) do not handle the calf too often, "let the dams lick and recognize the infant(s) properly".
- (s) watch dung and urine "for absence or for any abnormality".

**Ear tag.** NG-GLPP §11.4(a): "Permanent identification should be provided for each newborn rather with an ear tag." It gives no age. Penn State tags heifer calves in the newborn protocol, before the first colostrum. The app already gives each calf the next dairy Tag Number at Calving. **The Step is to put that number in her ear.**

**Record.** NG-GLPP §12.1.1.1(z) lists, for young stock: "calf ID number, sex of the calf, sire ID number, dam ID number, birth weight, weight at weaning, … age at weaning".

## 5. _Toxocara vitulorum_

**How it reaches the calf.**

- **Merck (Sept 2024):** in pregnant cattle, larvae "are mobilized late in pregnancy and passed via the milk to calves". Calves under 6 months are affected; eggs appear in calf dung "from 3 weeks of age". Heavy infections cause "diarrhea, weight loss, and death".
- **Roberts, Fernando & Sivanathan 1990** (abstract only, buffalo): larvae are in the milk mostly "from the day after calving and for a further five days. From day 9 onwards, very few larvae were found in the milk."
- **Roberts 1990** (abstract only): eggs first appeared at 22.3 ± 1.6 days, and treating at 3 days old showed "no pre-natal transmission".

**Why 10–16 days.** Roberts 1989 (abstract only, buffalo calves): transmission "occurs in all calves during the first 2 days after birth, decreases to 53% by 6 days, 10% by 8-9 days and 2% from Day 10 onwards". "Treatment of 10-16-day-old calves with an anthelmintic, which is effective against immature parasites, is recommended."

**Cattle, not only buffalo.** Roberts 1992 (abstract only) is titled for **bovine** calves: "Treatment of bovine calves 10-16 days old with an anthelmintic which is effective against immature _Toxocara vitulorum_ killed the parasites, there was no new infection and recontamination of the environment was precluded." Prevalence in the bovine calves was lower than in buffalo calves in the same area.

**Drugs.**

| Drug                  | Efficacy                                                                                       | Source                       |
| --------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------- |
| Pyrantel              | 97% immature, 100% mature                                                                      | Roberts 1989 (abstract only) |
| Levamisole            | 97% immature, 83% mature (oral)                                                                | Roberts 1989 (abstract only) |
| Febantel              | 100% on one farm, 35% on another                                                               | Roberts 1989 (abstract only) |
| Oxfendazole           | 89% mature                                                                                     | Roberts 1989 (abstract only) |
| Piperazine            | 42% immature, 57% mature, "reduced the infection to levels which were probably not pathogenic" | Roberts 1989 (abstract only) |
| Ivermectin 0.2 mg/kg  | 96.83% FECRT, buffalo calves, Bangladesh                                                       | Biswas 2022                  |
| Levamisole 7.5 mg/kg  | 94.23%                                                                                         | Biswas 2022                  |
| Albendazole 7.5 mg/kg | 85.84%                                                                                         | Biswas 2022                  |

Biswas 2022 treated calves with patent infections, so its figures are against mature worms. It says "albendazole, levamisole and ivermectin are most commonly used anthelmintics" in Bangladesh. Neither paper found gave fenbendazole's efficacy against _T. vitulorum_.

**What DLS lists.** NG-GLPP Appendix 35 (p. 270), "Round worms":

- "Piperazine (45mg/100ml) 30ml for cattle, buffalo, calves, sheep & goats, Oral, First dose to be given within 5-6 days of birth, Repeat at 45 days intervals".
- Also tetramisole 5 mg/kg, morantel citrate 10 mg/kg and levamisole 7.5 mg/kg.

**The DLS day 5–6 dose comes before the milk-borne infection ends** (about day 10, Roberts), **and uses the weakest drug.** A dose at about day 14 with a drug effective against immature worms follows the evidence. The Vet names the product and its withdrawal days (ticket 04).

**Bangladeshi cattle calves.**

- **Mustafa 2022, Table 2:** _Toxocara_ spp. in 50 of 200 calves (25.0%) against 15 of 400 adult cattle (3.75%). It was highest in summer.
- **Biswas 2021** (abstract only, buffalo calves): 22.9% overall, 51.7% at 1–3 months. Coastal regions, the rainy season and young age were the risk factors.
- **Mirsarai, Chattogram:** 22.66% of calves **[SNIPPET]**.
- **Islam 2015:** 43.5% of CIG farms first dewormed calves by 45 days, 56.5% later.
- **"Prevalence of Calf Diseases…" (unread):** "parasitic infestation (14.82%)" among calf diseases on three Bangladeshi farms **[SNIPPET]**.

## 7. Weaning and milk before it

**DLS: how much milk.** NG-GLPP §11.3 and §11.4:

- "Newborn should feed milk/milk replacer at 10% of its bwt/day for first 3 weeks of age."
- "During the first few weeks, milk should be fed to newborns at least 8-12 times a day."
- "Newborns should consume milk at 15% of their bwt/day after 3 weeks of age."
- "Concentrates should be introduced to calves at 3 weeks of age and high-quality hay from 5-8 weeks of age to aid in rumen development and prepare them to be weaned."

**DLS: when to wean (§12.1.1.1.1).**

- "Calves should intake calf starter at 1 kg/day for 3 days before weaning."
- Starter "must have 22-26% protein", though 18% suffices for Sahiwal calves.
- "When the calf intake 3 kg calf starter for at least a week, high-quality hay should be introduced."
- "Calves must have access to free choice water from day 3."
- "Weaning of calves should be based upon dry matter intakes. It is possible to wean a calf even at 4 weeks of age."

**DLS: early and natural weaning (§11.6.1).**

- "In the backyard and smallholders' farms, natural or late weaning is practiced … and early weaning is practiced in commercial farms."
- "The age of natural or late weaning in cattle is 8-10 months."
- Early weaning can be "at 30, 45, 60, 75 days after birth", or "after 4 months of age when the rumen development is complete (requires 3-4 months)".
- "It is recommended for early weaning of calves at 100-120 kg body weight for cattle."
- Calves and cows "begin to forget about each other between 7 and 14 days". §10.1.3.2(e), on dairy cows: "the calf should be weaned as early as possible", because suckling delays the dam's return to heat.

**Merck.** "slightly more than 4 L of milk or reconstituted milk replacer/day" for a 40–50 kg calf, in two feeds. "Solid feed should be introduced early (by 3 days old)", with starter at 18–20% crude protein.

**Penn State** feeds milk replacer to 6 weeks, once a day in week 7, and is "fully weaned by 8 weeks". Calves move to groups when "eating 4-5 lbs of grain per day".

**Bangladeshi practice.**

- Kamal 2010: "Generally suckling and weaning is not controlled in dairy production system in Bangladesh."
- Chowdhury 2017: 60% of Chattogram farms used suckling, and 72% fed waste milk.
- No survey found gives the age at which Bangladeshi dairy farms wean.

**90 days against DLS's 100–120 kg.** Registered 75% Friesian calves weighed 64.32 kg at three months and 99.06 kg at six (Rahman 2015). A calf weaned at 90 days is therefore well under DLS's 100–120 kg. **90 days is early weaning in DLS's sense.** It is sound only if the calf is eating concentrate: DLS's 1 kg of starter a day for 3 days, which Teagasc also uses ("one kilogram of concentrate for three consecutive days before weaning"). **The weigh-in at weaning should record the weight, not gate on it.**

**Milk for a 30 kg calf.** 3.0 L a day for weeks 1–3, rising towards 15% of her growing weight after that (my arithmetic). NG-GLPP's 15% is high by Merck's figure, which is about 9% for a 45 kg calf (my arithmetic). **A per-calf milk figure is the Owner's choice; the Vet may want the lower one.**

**Age.**

- Hossain 2013: "the mortality started to decrease gradually after their first month of life"; "Calves of first month of life may have higher mortality risk"; "age of the animal was found to be the most important [risk factor], before breed and season".
- Islam 2005: highest "in first month of life (35.2%)" **[SNIPPET]**.
- NG-GLPP §11.5(b): "the highest morbidity and mortality are seen in calves before weaning".
- Islam 2015: _Clostridium perfringens_ in 38.5% of diarrhoeic calves' samples within the first month.

**Season.**

- Hossain 2013: "Mortality rate were 28.99%, 33.03% and 37.98% in rainy, summer and winter season". Crossbreds died more in summer, indigenous calves more in winter.
- Islam 2005: "highest in monsoon (36.4%) followed by winter (34.6%) and summer (29.0%)" **[SNIPPET]**.
- The two farms disagree; **season is worth reporting, not assuming.**

**Breed.** Hossain 2013: "The mortality was significantly higher in exotic and crossbred animals than in local/indigenous calves."

**Benchmark.** NG-GLPP §11.5(a): "Above 10% of calf mortality is not acceptable, although about 5 to 25% mortality is reported for replacement animals in dairy farms." Teagasc aims for "Less than 10% calf morbidity". **The Bangladeshi studies (6.3–12.3%) sit either side of DLS's 10%.**

**The plan's figure.** The README's "calf morbidity of 30.9% and mortality of 8.64%, diarrhoea (10.2%) and pneumonia (6.5%)" is from **Tora et al. 2021**, 196 calves in Gamo Zone, **southern Ethiopia** (abstract only). It is not a Bangladeshi study. The Bangladeshi figures above should replace it.

## Unclear / not found

- **The "each hour of delay … 10%" figure.** It came up only in a search engine's summary, credited to "Bangladesh dairy farms". Europe PMC full-text search found no such sentence. Not used. The per-hour figure that was read is Van Camp's navel-infection OR of 1.15 (abstract only).
- **What Bangladeshi farms actually do at weaning.** No survey was found. DLS describes the options but reports no practice figures.
- **A deshi birth-weight range.** Only means and standard errors were found, so the 12 kg floor is a judgement.
- **Is 7% tincture of iodine sold in Bangladesh?** Not checked; povidone-iodine (usually 10% solution, 1% available iodine) is common in pharmacies. **The Vet should say which to use**, and whether chlorhexidine is the fallback. Teat dip is too weak (CalfCare.ca).
- **Piperazine at day 5–6 (DLS) or a better drug at 10–16 days (Roberts)?** The two disagree; this is the Vet's call.
- **Fenbendazole against _T. vitulorum_.** No efficacy figure found.
- **Second-feed volume.** Every source gives the timing; the volume (5% of birth weight, or the rest of NG-GLPP's 4 L) is my inference.
- **NG-GLPP's "suckle within 20 min of birth"** is tighter than any other source's standing times, and was not used as a deadline.
- **Two Bangladeshi calf-mortality papers** (Islam 2005; "Prevalence of Calf Diseases…") were reached only as snippets. Their full tables (age at death by month) were not read.
