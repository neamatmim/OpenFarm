# When a cow should show heat, and when her milk says she is ill

**Question:** OpenFarm will build a "heat watch" list and a "cows giving less" list. The Owner has accepted defaults pending this research: from 60 days after calving, any open cow with no heat in the last 24 days; a return to heat after a service expected on days 18–24; heifer first service at 18 months; and a milk drop of 20% over 2 days against her previous 7 days. On what evidence do these rest? By how many days after calving should a crossbred cow in Bangladesh have shown a heat, and what do Bangladeshi studies report for calving-to-first-heat and calving interval? How long is the estrous cycle, and what does a short or long return mean? At what age and weight should a crossbred heifer be served, and what do Bangladeshi heifers actually reach? What fall in a cow's daily milk points to mastitis, ketosis or lameness?

**Researched:** 30 September 2026.

## Answer

1. **Keep day 60 as the day a cow with no heat goes on the list. It is DLS's own figure, not a Bangladeshi average.**
   - **DLS says to act by day 60.** NG-GLPP says to "re-examine cows that don't come into heat at 50-60 days postpartum". Its breeding table gives "Calving to first heat 40 days" and "Calving to first service 60 days or less". Its target is "60-85 days open period" and "an inter-calving interval of 360 to 380 days".
   - **Merck:** voluntary waiting period "60–70 days for cows"; average days open "100–110".
   - **Bangladeshi research uses the same line.** Kamal et al. 2014 defined anestrus in crossbred cows "at =>60 days postpartum" (abstract only).
2. **Most Bangladeshi crossbred cows will be on that list at day 60, and many cows that are cycling will be among them.**
   - **First ovulation is late.** By milk progesterone, only 25 of 84 cows (29.7%) cycled within 120 days. HF crossbreds that did cycle began at 80.2 ± 17.5 days, and intensively managed cows at 73.5 ± 10 days (Saha 2015).
   - **Heats are late or missed.** In the AI program the median from calving to a seen estrus was 120 days. Heat was "accurately detected" in only 30% of cows, with 40% "not detected when they were in oestrus" (Shamsuddin 2006, abstract only).
   - **Surveys agree.** Farmers reported a post-partum heat at 114 days for HF × Local cows, 125 for Sahiwal × Local and 127 for Sindhi × Local (Famous 2021). Calving to first service averaged 153.4 ± 80.6 days (Siddiqui 2013, abstract only).
   - **Only 18% of cows past day 60 had no ovarian activity.** Kamal 2014 examined them by ultrasound (abstract only). **So a cow on the list is more often a missed heat than a cow that is not cycling.**
3. **Calving interval in Bangladeshi crossbreds is about 13–14 months, against DLS's 12.**
   - **CCBDF daughters (smallholder herds):** 12.97–13.69 months, which is 394–416 days (my arithmetic); days open 101–110 (Hridoy 2025).
   - **Kishoreganj:** 410 days for HF crosses and 426 days for Sahiwal and Sindhi crosses (Famous 2021).
   - **Older reviews:** a range of "365-536 days" (Kamal 2010; Rahman 2021).
4. **The cycle is 18–24 days, averaging 21. The return window of days 18–24 is right.**
   - **DLS:** "21±3 days" for crossbreds; "17-24 days" for indigenous cattle.
   - **Merck:** "Cyclic changes occur in the ovary over 18–24 days"; "Intervals between breedings: 18–24 days for 65% of cows".
   - **What DLS says to do:** watch a served cow "every 3 weeks after breeding (3, 6, 9 weeks)".
   - **The UK data put the peak one day later:** the most common interval between services was 22 days, not 21 (Remnant 2015, abstract only).
5. **"No heat in the last 24 days" matches the top of the cycle.** A cycling cow unseen by day 25 has most likely had a heat that nobody saw. **24 fits both DLS (21 + 3) and Merck (18–24).** The abstracts do not report how many intervals fall on day 25; Remnant 2015 counted intervals of 15–30 days as the first return.
6. **What an off-window return means.** These are conventional readings, not diagnoses; one research abstract doubts the embryo-loss link.
   - **Under 18 days:** the service was probably not at a true heat, or she has follicular cysts, which Merck links to "shortened cycles".
   - **25–35 days (Viking gives 25–45):** usually read as the embryo dying after the cow had registered the pregnancy (Viking Genetics). Merck: 5–10% of pregnancies are lost from about day 14–19 to day 42.
   - **36–48 days:** one heat missed. The thesis abstract uses the same bands (Remnant 2019).
   - **Longer:** cysts or anestrus (Viking; Merck).
   - **Early loss hides in normal returns.** 35–40% of embryos are lost before day 14–19, and that loss brings the cow back in 18–24 days, like a failed service (Merck).
7. **Heifer first service: 18 months for crossbreds is DLS's figure, not what Bangladeshi farms reach. It should also check weight, and deshi heifers need their own age.**
   - **DLS:** crossbreds "18-20 months", at "180-275 kg". §12.1.1.1 wants "about 250 and 300 kg at 18 months", and conception "when 18 to 21 months old". Indigenous heifers: 30 months, 150–250 kg.
   - **Abroad:** Merck puts breeding at 13–15 months, at "a minimum of 50–55%" of mature weight.
   - **Bangladeshi crossbred cows weigh 433–493 kg** on commercial farms (Rahman 2024). So 55–60% of that is **238–296 kg** (my arithmetic), close to DLS's 250 kg.
   - **Field reality is later.** Age at first conception was 19.7–22.2 months among CCBDF bulls' daughters (Hridoy 2025). Age at first fertile service was 24.2 months for HF crosses in Kishoreganj (Famous 2021).
8. **No study or published software gives "20% over 2 days" as a disease threshold. It is a convention.**
   - **Closest research definition:** a "perturbation" is at least 5 days below the expected curve, with at least 1 day under 80% of it (Adriaens 2021, abstract only). These happened 3.8 times per lactation, and "Only 1% of the lactations had no perturbations". Many are not disease.
   - **Disease drops build over days.** Yield falls 5–7 days before ketosis is diagnosed (Edwards & Tozer 2004). With clinical mastitis it falls "1 or 2 wk before diagnosis" (Gröhn 2004), and the drop starts on average "8.7 days before the first treatment" (Adriaens 2021 PVM). All abstract only.
   - **Lameness and subclinical mastitis are slow.** Lameness lowers yield "from up to 4 mo before" diagnosis (Green 2002, abstract only). Subclinical mastitis costs 0.35–1.09 kg a day at 200,000 cells/mL (Hand 2012, abstract only).
   - **So 20% in 2 days will catch sudden illness,** such as milk fever, acute (often _E. coli_) mastitis, or ketosis and displaced abomasum. **It will miss subclinical mastitis and early lameness;** those need the CMT and a walk past the cows.
   - **Estrus also drops milk.** NG-GLPP lists a "Sharp decline in milk production" among standing-heat signs. **A cow on the milk-drop list should be checked against the heat list.**

## 1. When a cow should first show heat after calving

**DLS (NG-GLPP).**

- **§10.1.3.2(a)** (p. 151): "Total 305 days of lactation period, 45-60 days dry off, 60-85 days open period and calving almost at yearly interval are desirable for profitable dairy cow/farm operation."
- **§10.1.3.2(j)(2):** "Examine cows around 30-40 days postpartum to ascertain uterine health, and re-examine cows that don't come into heat at 50-60 days postpartum, and treat the cows, if necessary."
- **§10.1.3.2(j)(3):** "Inseminate all cows with proven bulls' semen on first heat by 40-60 days of calving."
- **§10.1.3.2(g):** "If the lactating cows don't conceive between 60 to 85 days postpartum, a Registered Veterinarian should be consulted".
- **Suckling and anestrus:**
  - (d) "lactational anestrus can occur for utilizing more body reserves in the early lactation".
  - (e) "the suckling stimulus has a negative effect on cyclicity of the dam … so the calf should be weaned as early as possible".
  - (h) "Presence of teaser bull during postpartum results in causing cows to cycle earlier."
- **§10.1.3.4(j)** (p. 154): good feeding, care and housing "will help the cows to come into heat within 60 days of calving".
- **§4.1.2.1.2 A.2** (p. 31), for a cow worth buying: "open days not more than 85 days after the previous calving" and "an inter-calving interval of 360 to 380 days".
- **Appendix 26** (p. 262, "Adopted from TNAU, 2022a"):
  - "Calving to first heat 40 days" for indigenous, exotic/cross and buffalo alike.
  - "Calving to first service 60 days or less".

**Merck.**

- **Performance targets (Edwards, July 2026):**
  - "Voluntary waiting period (VWP) 60–70 days for cows; 80–90 days for heifers".
  - "Days in milk at first breeding VWP + 11 days".
  - "Average days open 100–110 (ideally lower end of range to achieve 12-month calving)"; "Calving interval 13.5 months".
  - "Full uterine involution 40–45 days post partum".
- **Hormonal Control of Estrus:** cows "need to be at least 40–45 days postpartum" before synchronization.
- **Breeding Programs (Statham, September 2024):**
  - "Genuine anestrus is not common in dairy cows".
  - Silent heat "decreases as lactation progresses … the incidence of silent heat is low by 4 months postpartum".
  - Anestrous ovaries "may reveal no activity or change after several examinations during a period of 3 weeks". A common cause is "excessive loss of body weight postpartum in lactating dairy cows".
  - **Merck gives no day by which a cow with no heat must be examined.** That day comes from DLS (50–60).
- **An older extension guide:** "Most cows normally show their first estrus by 30-50 days after calving" (Smith & Becker 1994, NMSU; summarizing fetch). That figure is for Holstein herds in the United States.

Saha 2015 sampled milk every 10 days and stopped at day 120, so its day counts describe only the cows that cycled by then. It found that "cyclic cows showed progesterone peak(s) before showing behavioural oestrus". The first ovulation is often silent.

**Why so late.**

- **Body condition, weight and suckling.** In Saha's cows weighing 152–200 kg at calving only 5.2% cycled within 120 days, against 38.3% of those at 201–300 kg.
- Kamal 2014 (abstract only): the odds of true anestrus were "17.52" times higher with poor body condition, "2.82 times higher in suckled than in nonsuckled cows", and "2.53 times higher in cows that calved during the cold season".
- **Management.** Saha's own conclusion: HF crosses need "an intensive farming system with proper management and health care, otherwise it would be very difficult to achieve a calving interval of 365 days."

Hridoy's days-open column ranges from 50 to 240, so shorter intervals may have been excluded. **DLS's 60–85 days open and 360–380-day interval are targets that the averages in these studies do not reach.**

**For the app.** Day 60 is where DLS draws the line, and where Kamal 2014 starts calling a cow anestrous. **On a Bangladeshi farm the list at day 60 will be long.** It holds three kinds of cow:

- cows cycling unseen (Shamsuddin: 40% of heats missed);
- cows not yet cycling (Saha);
- a few truly anestrous (Kamal: 18%).

The list's words should ask for **closer watching and, per NG-GLPP, the Vet's examination**. They should not call the cow barren. A second mark at **day 85** would match DLS's "open days not more than 85" and §10.1.3.2(g) (my suggestion).

## 2. The estrous cycle and the return window

**Length.**

- **NG-GLPP §7.2.3.1.1(d)** (p. 78): "Estrus cycle length: 21±3 days for both the indigenous and cross breeds." Appendix 26 gives "17-24 days" for indigenous and "21±3" for exotic/crosses.
- **Merck:** "Cyclic changes occur in the ovary over 18–24 days" (Breeding Programs); "an estrous cycle (~21 days)" (Overview); "Length of estrous cycle 18–24 days" and "Intervals between breedings 18–24 days for 65% of cows" (performance targets).
- **UK herds** (abstracts only):
  - Remnant 2015, 42,252 cows: "a modal ISI of 22d"; heifers 21 d. "87% [of variation was] at the insemination level … cycle length varies substantially more between cycles within a cow than between cows or herds." "the normal range of ISI for modern UK dairy cows is longer than expected".
  - Greenham 2019, tail chalk, 6,092 cows: "Modal interestrus interval was 21 d … Interservice interval was significantly longer … with a modal value of 22 d".
- **Heat itself:** "12-18 hours" (NG-GLPP). "On average, estrus lasts 18 hours; often, however, it is appreciably shorter" (Merck).

**What DLS asks after a service (NG-GLPP §7.2.3.3, p. 83).**

- (e) "watch the bred cattle and buffalo carefully every 3 weeks after breeding (3, 6, 9 weeks)".
- (g) "check the bred cattle and buffalo from 45 to 60 days after breeding for pregnancy".
- (i) call the Vet "when she is found not pregnant after 60 days of breeding".
- (j) call the Vet for cows "not conceived after 3 services".
- (k) keep a 3-week heat calendar (Appendix 25, a 21-row table).
- **Merck** checks pregnancy "28–32 days after being bred", by ultrasound.

**18–24 is well supported; so is 24 for "overdue".** For deshi cows DLS's range starts a day earlier (17). The window could open at day 17 for them (my suggestion). It barely matters to a list.

**What an off-window return means.** Remnant's thesis (abstract only) names the standard bands. These readings are conventions in herd analysis, not per-cow diagnoses:

| Interval after service     | Band (Remnant 2019) | Usual reading                                                                                                                                                                                                        |
| -------------------------- | ------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 2–17 days                  | "short irregular"   | Served when not truly in heat, or cysts: "follicular cysts are associated with anestrus, nymphomania, and shortened cycles" (Merck)                                                                                  |
| 18–24 days                 | "short regular"     | Service failed, or the embryo died before the cow registered the pregnancy (Merck: 35–40% "from conception to maternal recognition of pregnancy at 14–19 days"). **These two cannot be told apart by the calendar.** |
| 25–35 days (Viking: 25–45) | "long irregular"    | "insemination was successful but that a high proportion resulted in early embryo loss" (Viking Genetics). Merck: 5–10% of pregnancies lost from recognition to day 42                                                |
| 36–48 days                 | "long regular"      | A heat was missed in between (twice the cycle)                                                                                                                                                                       |
| over 48 days               | "extended"          | Several heats missed, cysts ("irregular heat", Viking), anestrus, or later embryo loss                                                                                                                              |

**Caution.** A search summary carried the sentence, from a paper not identified, that "evaluations of interestrous intervals are not a good indicator of early embryonic death" **[SNIPPET]**. And Greenham's abstract found that "longer interservice interval length may be more conducive to subsequent conception". **The app can name the band; the Vet decides what it means for the cow.**

## 3. Heifer first service

**DLS.**

- **§7.2.3.1.1** (p. 78):
  - "a) Age at puberty: 24 months for indigenous and 12-15 months for cross breeds."
  - "b) Age at first breeding: 30 months for indigenous and 18-20 months for cross breed."
  - "c) Weight at first breeding: 150-250 kg for indigenous and 180-275 kg for cross breeds."
  - Appendix 26 repeats these and gives "Optimum weight at first mating" of "250 kg" for indigenous heifers.
- **§12.1.1.1** (pp. 165–166):
  - (l) "Heifers' weight should be monitored regularly and adjusted the feed with supplements to get the target weights for cross heifers about 250 and 300 kg at 18 months of age."
  - (q) "Cross breed heifers should be at first calving in 27-30 months old that means they conceived when 18 to 21 months old, so they should be maintained on adequate nutrition to ensure that they are fertile and cycling during 18 to 21 months of age."
- **§12.1.1.1.1(b)(8)** (p. 167): "Puberty for heifers is governed by size and weight, not age."
- **An inconsistency in DLS:** §12.1.1.1(c) says heifers should "get pregnant by 12-15 months". That contradicts (q) and §7.2.3.1.1, and reads like the international target copied in. **The 18–20-month figure is the one DLS repeats three times.**

**International targets (Merck).**

- **Van Saun (December 2025):** "desired age at first calving, generally between 22 and 24 months", and "50–55% of mature weight at 13–15 months old". "Heifers should grow to achieve a minimum of 50–55% and 80–85% of their mature weight at breeding and first calving, respectively."
- **Van Saun's weight table:** at 13–15 months, 53.7–61.1% of mature weight. That is 242–275 kg for "small breeds" and 295–336 kg for "medium breeds".
- **Statham:** "Lifetime profits of dairy replacement heifers are maximized when heifers calve at 23–25 months old."
- **Romano:** "at least 60% of the expected mature weight" before synchronization.

**What mature weight is for this farm's cows.**

- **Rahman, Chowdhury & Islam 2024**, three commercial Keraniganj farms: Holstein crossbred cows averaged **433 kg** (50–67.5% Holstein, the rest Sahiwal) and **493 kg** (75–87.5% Holstein).
  - 55–60% of 433 kg is 238–260 kg; of 493 kg it is 271–296 kg (my arithmetic).
  - **So DLS's 250 kg, and its 250–300 kg at 18 months, match the international percentage rule for these cows.**
- **Saha 2015**, mostly smallholder cows: HF crosses averaged 307.5 ± 65.5 kg at calving. Lighter herds would get a lower target.
- **Deshi and Red Chittagong mature weights** were not researched here.

**What Bangladeshi heifers reach.**

Hridoy's first conception ranged from 12 to 36 months in every group. The best-managed 75% HF daughters averaged under 20 months. **So conception at 18 months is reachable, but it is ahead of the average.**

**For the app.**

- **Crossbreds:** 18 months (about 548 days, my arithmetic) is DLS's lower bound, so the first service falls due there.
- **Deshi:** a deshi heifer at 18 months is far from DLS's 30. The list should not raise her until 30 months (about 913 days, my arithmetic), or it should key on breed.
- **Weight:** because "Puberty … is governed by size and weight, not age", a weigh-in near 18 months should show whether she has reached **about 250 kg** (DLS; 55% of a 433–493 kg cow). A light heifer's service should wait for the weight, not the calendar.
- **Too heavy:** "The heifers should not be over-conditioned" (§12.1.1.1.1(b)(7)).

## 4. How far a cow's milk must fall to mean illness

**DLS gives no number.**

- NG-GLPP names a milk decline as a sign of heat, of metritis ("decreased milk yield … within 21 days after parturition") and of mastitis, but sets no threshold.
- It asks for exactly the record the app keeps: "periodic milk yield" (Merck, _Health and Production Management Programs_) and, for a new cow, milk weighed "with a 12 hours interval … for at least 1-2 days" (NG-GLPP A.3).

**Published software keeps its thresholds to itself.**

- Lely's Horizon gives each cow a "sick chance rating from 0 to 99" from yield, conductivity and rumination **[SNIPPET]**. DeLaval's DelPro compares a cow with her "expected yield" **[SNIPPET]**.
- No maker's page read gave a percentage or a day count for its milk-drop alert. **The 20%/2-day rule cannot be matched to a commercial default.**

**Research definitions.**

- **Adriaens et al. 2021, JDS** (abstract only; 62,406 perturbations, 50 farms): "Perturbations were defined as periods of at least 5 d of negative residuals having at least 1 day that the total daily milk production was below 80% of the estimated unperturbed lactation curve." There were "3.8 ± 1.9" per lactation in 305 days, and "Only 1% of the lactations had no perturbations". The falling phase lasted "an average of 10.1" days, losing "2.3 kg of milk … per day".
- **Adriaens et al. 2021, _Prev Vet Med_** (abstract only; 4,553 treated mastitis cases): "In 62 % of the cases, we found a perturbation in milk yield at the cow level at the time of treatment. On average, perturbations started 8.7 days before the first treatment". Relative losses "were highest on the first treatment day".
- **Cabbia et al. 2026** (full text; 350 Simmental cows, automatic milking): a perturbation began when a short smoothed curve fell below a long one. The time from onset to the lowest yield was 13.4–17.8 days, depending on stage and stressor. Lameness gave "the longest (30.6 d …) perturbations".
- **Huybrechts et al. 2014** (abstract only): statistical control on yield residuals "had a sensitivity of 63% for detecting clinical mastitis".
- **Lukas et al. 2009** (abstract only): "Significant changes in MY and MEC were observed as early as 10 and 9 d before diagnosis." Their control charts were set at 98–99.5% specificity and "give fewer false-positive alerts than the disease detection system currently used on the farm". The abstract gives no percentage.

**How big the drop is, by disease.**

| Disease                     | Finding                                                                                                                                                                                          | Source (all abstract only) |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------- |
| Clinical mastitis           | "daily loss during the first 2 wk after the occurrence of mastitis varied from 1.0 to 2.5 kg"; cows "did not reach their premastitis milk yields" again                                          | Rajala-Schultz et al. 1999 |
| Clinical mastitis           | "Milk yield generally began to drop 1 or 2 wk before diagnosis; the greatest loss occurred immediately following diagnosis"                                                                      | Gröhn et al. 2004          |
| Subclinical mastitis        | at 200,000 cells/mL, "24-h milk loss ranged from 0.35 to 1.09 kg"; at 2,000,000, "1.49 to 4.70 kg"                                                                                               | Hand, Godkin & Kelton 2012 |
| Ketosis, displaced abomasum | "Milk yields were lower by 6, 7, and 5 d, respectively, before diagnoses of ketosis, left displaced abomasum, and digestive disorders"; sick cows gave "approximately 15 kg/d less" than healthy | Edwards & Tozer 2004       |
| Milk fever                  | "the greatest average daily effect was estimated for milk fever with a 10.36-kg decrease in MY"                                                                                                  | Lukas et al. 2009          |
| Lameness                    | "milk yield was reduced from up to 4 mo before a case of lameness was diagnosed … and for the 5 mo after"; about 360 kg per lactation                                                            | Green et al. 2002          |
| Lameness                    | "lameness significantly reduced daily milk yield (by approximately 1.3 kg/d)"                                                                                                                    | Khorrami et al. 2026       |

These herds give far more milk than this farm's cows: 6.6–8.6 L a day for CCBDF daughters (Hridoy 2025), 12–14 kg on Keraniganj commercial farms (Rahman 2024). **The kilogram losses cannot be turned into percentages for these cows.**

**So is 20% over 2 days right?**

- **It is a convention, not a measured threshold.**
- **The 20% depth matches Adriaens's research definition** (a day under 80% of expected), but Adriaens asks for 5 days, not 2, and compares with a fitted lactation curve, not the last 7 days.
- **The diseases that make a sharp drop are the ones it will catch:** acute mastitis, milk fever, ketosis, displaced abomasum. In an activity-and-rumination system these reached a 91–98% alert rate, 1.6–3 days before diagnosis, for ketosis and displaced abomasum (Stangaferro Part I, abstract only). Mastitis was caught only 58% of the time, best for _E. coli_ (Part II).
- **It will miss subclinical mastitis and early lameness**, whose losses are below 20% and build over weeks.

**Three practical points (my reading).**

- **Keep the 2 days.** On a farm that writes yields by hand, one wrong or missed entry makes a false 50% drop. Two days is the guard against it.
- **Check the heat list too.** A heat can drop milk for a day (NG-GLPP: "Sharp decline in milk production"). So can weaning or a change in suckling, since many Bangladeshi cows are milked with the calf present (Saha 2015).
- **Tune the rule on the farm's own data.** The farm will learn how many alerts 20% raises. A gentler companion rule is possible, for example below 90% of the 7-day average for 5 days, but that is my suggestion and no source measures it. It would catch the slow falls.

## Unclear / not found

- **A Bangladeshi threshold for "no heat after calving" other than DLS's 50–60 days.** The studies report averages; none sets a day for action. Kamal 2014's "≥60 days" is a study definition.
- **The full text of Kamal 2014,** which would say how many of the 82% of cows past day 60 that were not truly anestrous were cycling unseen, and at what day. Only the abstract was read.
- **How many returns fall on day 25 and later in Bangladeshi cows.** Remnant's modal 22 days is from the UK. No Bangladeshi interval distribution was found.
- **A Merck page named "Estrous Cycles in Cattle".** It does not exist under that name. The cycle facts are on the pages cited.
- **Any herd-software milk-drop default** (Lely, DeLaval, Afimilk, DairyComp). Not published in pages that could be read.
- **Any study testing "20% in 2 days against 7 days"** for sensitivity or false alarms. None found. Adriaens's 5-day/80% rule describes perturbations. It was not validated as a disease alert.
- **Percentage losses for low-yielding crossbred cows.** Every mastitis, ketosis and lameness loss found is in kilograms from high-yielding herds.
- **Mature weight and first-service age for deshi and Red Chittagong heifers on this kind of farm.** Only DLS's figures were used.
- **"Evaluations of interestrous intervals are not a good indicator of early embryonic death"** came only from a search summary; the paper behind it was not identified.
